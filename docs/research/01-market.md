# 1. The Market

## Tuition is almost universal

Sri Lanka has had free state education since the 1940s, yet private tuition has grown alongside it until it became a normal part of school life.

| Measure | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Secondary students who attend tuition | about 80%; some sources say 89% | Wikipedia, Sunday Times |
| University students who attended tuition for A/L | 84.9%; 96% in STEM | Research cited in Wikipedia |
| Urban households using tuition (2016) | 65%, up from 41% in 1995 | IPS / UNESCO |
| Rural households using tuition (2016) | 62%, up from 19% in 1995 | IPS / UNESCO |
| Estate-sector households (2016) | 36%, up from 15% in 1995 | IPS / UNESCO |
| Households reporting tuition spending | 13.9% (1990/91) → 54.2% (2012/13) | Household surveys |
| Tuition's share of household education spending | about 43–48% (the largest single item) | IPS, 2019 HIES |

Rural use has nearly caught up with urban use. That matters for us: the customers are not just in Colombo.

## How big is it in money?

Sources disagree a lot:

- Some news articles say **Rs. 16 billion** a year.
- Wikipedia, citing research, says **over Rs. 122 billion** a year, about 40% of what the government spends on education.
- Other articles say **Rs. 210 billion**.

The real number probably depends on the year and on what is counted. Whichever is right, the industry is large. **But** the money parents pay to teachers is not our market. Our market is what teachers and institutes will pay for **software**, which is a tiny fraction of it (see [02](02-competitors.md)).

## What tuition looks like on the ground

Four main formats:

1. **Mass classes ("hall classes"):** popular A/L teachers lecture to hundreds or even thousands of students in large halls. These are run like businesses, with staff, assistants and printed notes ("tutes").
2. **Institutes:** a building that rents its halls to many teachers. The institute collects fees or takes a share, handles the front desk and often issues student cards.
3. **Small groups:** 5–15 students in a teacher's home or a small room.
4. **Individual and online:** one-to-one or over Zoom. Online grew a lot during COVID-19 and stayed.

Each format needs something different:

| Format | Main pain | Who pays for software |
|---|---|---|
| Mass class | Checking that hundreds of students have paid this month, fast, at the door | The teacher; often already uses a system |
| Institute | Many teachers, many classes, splitting money between teachers and the institute | The institute owner |
| Small group | Remembering who has paid; chasing late fees | The teacher, but very price-sensitive |
| Online | Access control: who can join the Zoom or watch recordings | The teacher |

## Fees parents pay

These are typical monthly fees seen in class listings in 2026:

- A/L group classes: about **Rs. 2,000–5,000 per subject per month**, sometimes more for star teachers
- Online group classes: about Rs. 1,600–6,000 per month
- Total family spending per child: about Rs. 3,000–7,000 a month for lower-income families, up to Rs. 18,000–20,000 for higher-income families

**Important for us:** most single class fees are **under Rs. 5,000**. That is below the LankaQR zero-fee limit (see [03](03-sri-lanka-context.md)).

## The "exercise book" is real, but it is changing

The original pitch says tuition is "mostly managed in exercise books". That is still true for many small teachers. But the bigger teachers and institutes have moved on. Many already use:

- A **class card**, a paper or PVC card stamped or punched each month to show the fee is paid
- Barcode or RFID cards with a desktop system
- WhatsApp groups for announcements

So the real competitor is often **not** the exercise book. It is either (a) an existing system or (b) "good enough": a WhatsApp group plus a notebook, which costs nothing.

## Trends that help us

- **Phones everywhere.** Smartphone use is very high and WhatsApp has about 15 million users in Sri Lanka.
- **Cheaper digital payments.** LankaQR is now free for merchants on small payments.
- **After COVID, people are used to online classes**, so teachers accept software more readily.

## Trends that are risks

- **Economic pressure.** After the 2022 crisis, families struggle with fees (Sunday Times, 2023). Teachers in turn resist extra costs.
- **Regulation.** In December 2024 the Western Province tried to ban teachers from giving paid tuition to students from their own school. The ban was suspended, but it could come back. Some provinces have also tried to limit Sunday and Poya day classes. See [03](03-sri-lanka-context.md).
- **Falling student numbers.** This needs more research. Birth rates are falling, so the number of O/L and A/L students will shrink slowly over time.
